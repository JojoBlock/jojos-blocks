Starting a Print-on-Demand Shop with AI Design Help: What Beginners Should Know First

AI Tools & Online Business

By JoJo's Blocks · 2026-11-18 · 10 min read · print-on-demand, ai-design

Print on demand sounds like the perfect beginner business: you upload a design, someone else prints it on a t-shirt or mug, and you keep the difference. No stock, no money spent before a sale. That part is true. What the short videos leave out is the margin — often a few dollars per shirt — and that a design nobody can find earns nothing, however good it looks on your screen.

AI can help you move faster: generating ideas, drafting text for a mug, or making a mockup. It cannot judge what is worth selling or whether a phrase is someone else's trademark. This guide follows one t-shirt from idea to sale, with honest numbers at every step. No income promises.

How print on demand actually works

A customer orders a product with your design on it. A print provider prints that one item, packs it, and ships it. You never touch the product. You pay the base cost and shipping, plus the platform's charges, and keep what is left.

Route 1 — a marketplace, such as Redbubble. You upload designs, Redbubble puts them on dozens of products, and buyers find them through its own search. You set a markup — the default is 10% — and that is your margin. You do no marketing, but the margin is small and you have little control over price.

Route 2 — a fulfillment provider plus your own shop, such as Printify or Printful connected to Etsy or Shopify. You choose the products and provider, set your own price, and sell through a shop you control. The margin is usually higher, but you must bring the buyers yourself.

Step 1: Pick a narrow niche you can design for

"Funny t-shirts" is not a niche. "Short, warm phrases for new grandparents who garden" is. A narrow niche tells you what to design next, what words a buyer would search for, and keeps you away from the most crowded searches.

Pick a niche from something you know, then check it before you design anything. Search the marketplace for the words a buyer would type and look at how many results appear, what they cost, and what the best sellers look like. You are not looking for an empty market — that often means nobody wants the product. Look for a gap: a simpler version, a narrower audience, or a phrase nobody has done well yet.

Write it down in one sentence before you open any AI tool. Ours: "Simple, calm gardening phrases for new grandparents, on mugs and t-shirts."

Step 2: Make the design with AI help — then check it like a printer would

Start with your own idea, not the tool's. Write down the exact words, style, and feeling first. "A simple line drawing of a watering can with the words 'Grow slow, grow steady' in a plain serif font, on a transparent background" is a brief. "Make me a gardening design" is not a brief, and the tool will give you a generic one.

Generate the image, then check the words letter by letter. AI tools still misspell words, and a misspelled mug is unsellable. Ideogram is the strongest common tool for text inside an image: its free plan gives 30 credits a week, but only for accounts signed up with Google or Apple — Microsoft and email sign-ups get no free credits unless they later link a Google or Apple sign-in. Everything made on the free plan is public. Plus costs $20 a month, or $15 a month billed yearly. Check the text at full size, letter by letter.

Prepare the file properly. Most providers want a transparent PNG at 300 DPI — commonly 4500 × 5400 pixels for a t-shirt print area. A low-resolution image, or one with a white box behind it, prints badly. Ideogram's free plan downloads transparent images at 1K size; a larger file for print may need a paid plan or purchased credits.

Keep the design yours. AI output may not be unique, and you cannot sell a design that copies someone else's work. Do not ask AI to recreate a famous character, logo, or sports team's branding "in different words" — that is still copying, and marketplaces remove listings for it. For a fuller guide to image tools and licenses, see AI Image Generation for Beginners.

Step 3: Choose a platform and a provider — and order a sample

On the fulfillment route, the provider decides your cost, quality, and shipping time. Base costs vary by provider, garment, color, and size, so check the catalog before you set a price.

Printify is a marketplace of print providers, which usually means lower base costs. Its published start for a standard Bella+Canvas 3001 t-shirt is about $10.98, with US shipping around $4.45. Premium costs $39 a month, or $299 a year (about $24.99 a month), and takes up to 20% off most products.

Printful prints in its own facilities, with more consistent quality, and charges more: about $12.95 for the same shirt, shipping around $4.69. Its Growth plan costs $24.99 a month and becomes free once your yearly sales reach $12,000.

Redbubble works differently: no base cost, no provider to choose. It sets the base price, you add your markup, and it handles printing, shipping, and customer service. The catch is the tier system. Redbubble assigns each account a tier — Standard, Premium, or Pro — based on the quality and uniqueness of the work, not on sales alone, so new sellers are not automatically placed on Standard. Since September 2025 the platform fees are: Standard keeps 50% of monthly earnings, Premium 20%, Pro none. A further 50% fee applies to earnings from markup above 20%, and total fees are capped at $150 a month.

Whichever route you choose, order one sample of your best design before you promote it. It costs one base price plus shipping, and gives you a real photo for your listing.

Step 4: Follow the money on one t-shirt

A standard graphic t-shirt usually sells for $24.99 to $29.99. Below $22 little margin is left, and above $32 sales slow unless you have a real brand. Here is what one sale actually leaves, using a Bella+Canvas 3001 sold on Etsy for $24.99, on the free plans, with US shipping included in the price — so the Etsy fees below are worked on the $24.99 item price only.

Base product cost$10.98$12.95
Shipping$4.45$4.69

What you keep$6.74 (27%)$4.53 (18%)

That margin is before advertising, software, or your time. The Etsy fees in the table are the $0.20 listing fee, the 6.5% transaction fee, and 3% + $0.25 processing for US sellers. Offsite Ads add 12% or 15% when they bring the sale.

On Redbubble the margin is thinner. A $20 base-price t-shirt at 20% markup earns a $4 margin — about $2 after the Standard tier's 50% fee. At that rate, $100 a month takes roughly 50 shirt sales. Do not advertise until you know your margin per sale.

Step 5: List it properly, and tell buyers what they are getting

Buyers cannot touch the product, so the listing must answer their questions. Use real photos and honest mockups — flat and worn, plus your sample photo if you have one. Do not use an AI-generated photo of a person wearing the shirt as if it were real.

Write the title the way a buyer searches: "Grow Slow Grow Steady Gardening Mug for Grandma" will be found; "Cute Mug Design 7" will not. Say what is included and what is not — product, colors, sizes, material, delivery time — and that the item is printed to order, so the buyer understands the wait. On Etsy, add your production partner. Use tags for the niche: ten honest tags about gardening and grandparents beat thirty generic ones.

And remember Etsy's October 2026 AI rules: AI-generated art is allowed when you played a clear creative role and you say in the description that AI was used, but AI-generated patterns, recipes, and instructions are not allowed, and listings made by bots with no human involvement are banned. Check the Seller Handbook before you list.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Uploading a hundred designs and waiting. Ten designs in one niche, listed properly, teach you more than a hundred scattered ones.
  • Pricing from the retail price, not the margin. Work backwards from base cost, shipping, and fees — the table in Step 4, not the price tag.
  • Skipping the sample. A design that prints badly is not a product yet, whatever it looked like on screen.
  • Using trademarked words to get found. Borrowing a brand name or sports team can get a listing removed, and repeat removals can close an account.
  • Treating AI output as finished. Your judgment — the niche, the words, the checking — is what makes the design yours.

Conclusion

Print on demand lets you sell physical products without holding stock, but it is a slow, thin-margin business for most beginners. A few dollars per shirt, a marketplace fee on top, and thousands of competing designs mean success comes from a narrow niche, a few well-made designs, honest pricing, and listings that answer a buyer's questions. AI makes design work faster, but it does not choose the niche, check the spelling, or earn a buyer's trust. Start with one niche, five to ten designs, one platform, and one sample order. If a design sells, make more for the same buyer; if nothing sells after a few months, change the niche or the designs, not the number of uploads.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need money to start a print-on-demand shop?⌄

No. All three platforms have free plans, and you pay the base cost only when an order comes in. The one cost worth paying is a sample order.

Which platform is best for a beginner — Redbubble, Printify, or Printful?⌄

Redbubble is the simplest start, but the margin is small, about $2 a shirt on a Standard account. Printify and Printful keep more per sale but need you to bring the buyers. Start with one, not all three.

How much can I earn from print on demand?⌄

There is no reliable figure. Most beginners earn nothing at first, then small amounts. A t-shirt typically leaves $4 to $7 after costs on a fulfillment platform, about $2 on Redbubble's Standard tier. Treat any income as possible, never guaranteed.

Can I use AI-generated designs commercially?⌄

It depends on the tool's terms. Ideogram's terms say you may use generated images for any purpose, including commercial use, and Ideogram does not claim ownership of your output. But output may not be unique, and no tool's terms protect you if the design copies someone else's trademark or copyrighted work. Read the terms before you sell.
