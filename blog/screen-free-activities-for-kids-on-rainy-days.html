Screen-Free Activities for Kids on Rainy Days

By JoJo's Blocks · 2026-11-15 · 6 min read · screen-free activities, rainy day activities, indoor activities

Rain is tapping on the window, the park is out of the question, and the tablet is already in your child's hand. A rainy day does not have to mean a screen day. With a few simple ideas, you can fill a wet afternoon with coloring, building, dancing, and stories — no big setup and no screens needed. Here are 15 screen-free activities for kids ages 2 to 7, grouped by type, so you can pick one that fits the moment.

1. Why screen-free time matters on a rainy day

Rainy days are when screens win most easily. Everyone is stuck inside, energy is high, and the tablet is the fastest way to get some quiet. A little screen time is not a failure — but a whole rainy afternoon of it can crowd out the play, movement, reading, and conversation young children need most. The American Academy of Pediatrics' January 2026 policy, “Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents,” takes a different approach from the old advice. For years, the Academy suggested about an hour a day of high-quality programming for ages 2 to 5, watched together with an adult. The 2026 policy sets no single time limit. Instead, it asks families to think about the 5 Cs: the Child, the Content, Calm, Crowding Out, and Communication — who is watching, what they are watching, and what screens are crowding out.

For families who want a number, the policy notes limits might range under an hour a day for toddlers and preschoolers — a flexible example, not a rule. The World Health Organization still gives a 1-hour guideline for ages 2 to 4 — and less is better. Play is what fills that time best: the National Association for the Education of Young Children notes that play and learning go together, and engaged play can help with curiosity, initiative, problem solving, and focused attention. ZERO TO THREE agrees — young children learn best through hands-on, face-to-face interaction, and screen time should never replace real-world engagement.

2. Quiet and creative — coloring, drawing, and stories

Start here when energy is low or you need a calm stretch. These three activities need almost nothing.

  • 1. Coloring and drawing. Put out crayons, paper, and a few coloring pages. Coloring gives small hands practice with grip and control, and talking about the picture together can help with new words. Try free coloring pages, or a letter page from My First ABC Book — A is for Apple, B is for Ball.
  • 2. Story time, with voices. Read a favorite picture book aloud with silly voices for each character. Then ask your child to retell the story, or to draw their favorite part. A short talk after the story can be just as valuable as the story itself.
  • 3. Simple paper crafts. Fold paper airplanes and see whose flies farthest, or cut paper snowflakes. Keep the steps short, and let your child stop whenever they lose interest.

3. Build and pretend — forts, dress-up, and puppet shows

When your child has energy to burn but the rain keeps you inside, building and pretending can fill an hour.

  • 4. Blanket fort. Drape blankets over chairs or a table, add cushions and a flashlight, and you have a reading den or a spaceship. Let your child decide what the fort is — the pretending is the point.
  • 5. Dress-up and pretend play. A box of old clothes, hats, and scarves becomes a shop, a kitchen, or a doctor's office. Pretend play lets children practice new words and ideas.
  • 6. Puppet show. Put a sock on your hand, draw a face on a paper bag, and put on a show. Keep it short and silly, and let your child be the director.

4. Move your body — dance, obstacle courses, and tossing games

Rainy days trap a lot of wiggles indoors. These activities use that energy up.

  • 7. Dance party. Put on three favorite songs and dance together — freeze when the music stops. The freeze game gives practice at listening and stopping.
  • 8. Indoor obstacle course. Crawl under a blanket, hop between cushions, and walk along a line of tape on the floor. Keep it simple and change one part each round.
  • 9. Soft-ball toss. Toss a soft beach ball back and forth, and count how many catches you can make in a row. For younger children, roll the ball instead of throwing it.

5. Kitchen and hands-on — baking, dough, and sorting games

The kitchen is a rainy-day classroom, and the snack at the end is its own reward.

  • 10. Baking together. Let your child stir the batter, pour with help, and press cookie cutters into dough. Talk as you go — “two cups of flour” is counting practice in disguise.
  • 11. Play dough and sensory play. Roll, squeeze, and cut play dough, or fill a tray with dry rice and cups for scooping and pouring. Calm, hands-on play for a good stretch.
  • 12. Sorting and counting games. Put out buttons, pasta shapes, or clean socks, sort them by color or size, then count each pile together. It can help with early math ideas.

6. Thinking games — scavenger hunts, puzzles, and memory

When the afternoon starts to drag, a game with a goal can pull everyone back together.

  • 13. Indoor scavenger hunt. Write or draw a short list — something red, something soft, something round, a book about animals — and send your child hunting around the house. Draw the clues for children who cannot read yet.
  • 14. Puzzles. A wooden puzzle for a toddler or a 24-piece puzzle for a preschooler can fill a quiet half hour. Work on it together and let your child find the edge pieces first.
  • 15. Memory matching game. Lay a few pairs of cards face down — animal cards, color cards, or matching socks — and take turns turning two over. Start with four pairs and add more as they get the idea.

7. How to plan a rainy afternoon without a battle

You do not need all 15 activities. Two or three, picked well, can fill a whole rainy afternoon.

  • Offer a choice, not an order. “Fort or dance party?” works better than “Go and play.”
  • Start together. Many children need five minutes of you playing alongside them before they will happily play alone.
  • Keep sessions flexible. About 10 to 20 minutes suits many preschoolers, and less suits many toddlers — and let your child stop whenever they lose interest.
  • Rotate, do not repeat. Put the coloring pages away after one round and bring out the puzzles later.
  • Keep a rainy-day box ready: crayons, paper, a few books, and a blanket in one box.
  • End with something calm. A story or a coloring page before dinner can help everyone slow down.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a rainy-day activity last?⌄

About 10 to 20 minutes suits many preschoolers, and less suits many toddlers — but these are flexible ideas, not rules. Let your child stop whenever they lose interest. Stop while it is still fun, and a short happy session can be just as valuable as a long one.

What if my child only wants the tablet?⌄

That is normal, especially on a rainy day when screens are the easiest option. Offer a choice between two activities instead of an open question, start the activity together for a few minutes, and keep the tablet out of sight while you play. If screens do come out, watch together and talk about what you see.

What do I need to prepare for these activities?⌄

Very little: paper, crayons, blankets, cushions, a few books, and things you already have in the kitchen. A rainy-day box with crayons, paper, and a few favorite books means you are ready before the rain starts.
