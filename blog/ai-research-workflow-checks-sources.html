An AI Research Workflow That Checks Its Sources

AI Tools & Online Business

By JoJo's Blocks · 2026-11-18 · 10 min read · ai-research, fact-checking

Ask an AI assistant a research question and you will get an answer in seconds — confident, well written, and full of citations. That speed is real, and so is the problem underneath it: a citation is not proof. The link may open, the page may exist, and the sentence beside it may still say something the page never said.

That is not a reason to stop using AI for research. Use it the way a careful researcher would: let the AI do the slow work of finding and sorting material, then check the claims that matter yourself, against the original source, before you trust, quote, or publish them.

What AI research tools actually do

There are two kinds of AI research, and beginners often confuse them.

The first is web research: you ask a question, the tool searches the web, reads some pages, and writes an answer with links. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Perplexity all do this, and their "deep research" modes do it more thoroughly — they plan a search, run several rounds, and produce a longer report, usually in five to thirty minutes.

The second is research inside your own files: you upload documents — PDFs, articles, your own notes — and ask questions about them. Google's NotebookLM, renamed Gemini Notebook in July 2026, works this way. It answers only from the material you gave it, and points to the exact passage each claim comes from. It cannot invent a source that was never in your pile, though it can still misread one.

Neither kind replaces reading.

Step 2: Ask a question the tool can actually answer

A vague question gets a vague answer, and a vague answer is hard to check. Before you ask, write the question down in one sentence, and add three things that make it checkable.

The decision it serves. "What is the best email tool?" has no answer. "Which email tool under $30 a month lets a beginner send a weekly newsletter to under 1,000 subscribers?" can be answered and checked.

The facts you need, named. Prices, dates, limits, names.

What counts as a good source. Ask for primary sources where they exist: the vendor's own pricing page, the official help center, the regulator's own site. Add this to your prompt: "Answer with citations. Prefer primary sources. If a fact comes only from a secondary source, say so. If you cannot verify a fact, say that instead of guessing."

That last instruction matters most: AI models would rather guess than say they do not know.

A worked trace: follow one claim to its source

Here is the routine in action, on a claim you might actually meet: "Perplexity Pro costs $17 a month."

  1. Read the answer, then open the link. The AI cites Perplexity's Pro page. Open it yourself. It says $17 a month billed annually. That is a real number — on that page.
  2. Open a second source. Perplexity's pricing hub lists Pro at $20 a month. Both pages are official, and both are current. The difference is the billing basis: $20 is the monthly rate, $17 is the monthly equivalent of a $200 yearly bill.
  3. Write down what you actually know. "Pro is $20 a month, or $200 a year — $17 a month equivalent."

Step 3: Read the answer like a skeptic, not a fan

When the answer arrives, read it for the claims you would repeat to someone else — those are the ones that need checking.

Separate facts from framing. "This tool is popular" is framing. "This tool costs $20 a month" is a fact. Circle the facts: prices, dates, numbers, names, and anything you would put in a blog post or a decision about money.

Check the date of every source. A pricing page from 2024 is not evidence about a price in 2026.

Watch for the three classic errors. First, a real link attached to a claim the page does not support — the most common failure in independent tests. Second, a plausible-sounding study, quote, or statistic that does not exist at all. Third, a number that is close but wrong — a price from the wrong plan, a figure from the wrong region, a date from the wrong year. Close-but-wrong is the hardest kind to catch.

Do not trust confidence or length. A long, detailed report with many citations can still be wrong. The studies below disagree with each other on the exact rate, because they tested different tools on different tasks — do not average them.

What the studies actually found — kept separate

Each study below used a different method and tested different things. Read them as separate warnings, not as one combined score.

  • Liu, Zhang, and Liang (2023): in an audit of four generative search engines, only 51.5% of generated sentences were fully supported by their citations.
  • Tow Center, Columbia (March 2025): eight AI search tools were given excerpts from real news articles and asked to identify the source. Collectively they got more than 60% of the 1,600 queries wrong. ChatGPT Search was wrong on 134 of 200 and declined to answer none of them; Perplexity did best at 37% wrong, Grok 3 worst at 94%.
  • Walters and Wilder (2023): in 84 short literature reviews written by ChatGPT, 55% of GPT-3.5's references and 18% of GPT-4's references were fabricated outright.
  • DeepTRACE, Salesforce (September 2025): citation accuracy of 40–80% across deep-research systems.
  • EBU/BBC (October 2025): 45% of AI answers about news had a significant issue, and 31% had serious sourcing problems.
  • Onweller and colleagues, Cited but Not Verified (May 2026): factual accuracy of 39–77% across 14 models, even though more than 94% of the links worked.

Step 4: Check the three claims that matter most

You do not need to verify every sentence — just the three kinds of claim that cause real damage when they are wrong.

1. Anything involving money. Prices, fees, limits, refunds. Open the vendor's own pricing page yourself and find the number. Prices change often — Perplexity's Pro price has been listed at both $17 a month billed yearly and $20 a month billed monthly, as the worked trace above shows — so check the page on the day you need the figure.

2. Anything you will quote or publish. A statistic, a study, a quote, a legal rule. Open the source and read the sentence the AI is relying on. If the page does not contain it, the claim is unverified, however confident the answer sounded. If the claim is a study, check that it exists: search for its title, its authors, and its DOI, and confirm the DOI belongs to that study in a registry such as Crossref. A DOI that belongs to a different paper is a clear sign the reference was invented.

3. Anything that changes a decision. If the answer would make you buy a tool, cancel a service, or tell a client something, check it against a second, independent source. One source is a lead. Two sources that agree — ideally one of them primary — is a finding. If two good sources disagree, say so in your notes.

Step 5: Keep your sources, so you can prove what you found

Research you cannot retrace is research you cannot defend. Keep a short source list for anything you will use again: the question you asked, the tool and date, and the three or four primary sources you actually opened, with the date you opened them. A link is not enough — pages change. Copy the sentence or number you relied on into your notes, with the page name and date. Keep the files themselves if you use NotebookLM, so its answers can still point back to the passages they came from.

What it costs, and when paying is worth it

You can do serious research for $0: ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity all offer web search and some deep research free, Claude offers web search free, and NotebookLM is free with a Google account. US prices were checked in November 2026, before tax; check the vendor's pricing page before you pay.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Quoting a citation you never opened. A link that works is not a claim that is true.
  • Trusting a report because it is long. Length is not evidence.
  • Accepting a study, quote, or statistic without checking it exists. Search for the title, the authors, and the DOI before you repeat it.
  • Using an old source for a current fact. Prices, plans, and limits change. Check the date on the page.
  • Asking one tool and stopping. If a claim would change a decision, check it against a second, independent source.
  • Pasting private information into a research tool. Customer details, bank numbers, and confidential documents do not belong in a prompt.

Conclusion

AI research tools are at their best on the slow, mechanical jobs: searching widely, reading quickly, and sorting what they found into a shape you can work with. You still write the question, decide which claims matter, open the sources, and check the facts before anyone else relies on them.

Frequently asked questions

Which AI research tool should a beginner start with?⌄

The one you already have. ChatGPT or Gemini for web questions, Perplexity if you want sources shown up front, Claude for long documents, and NotebookLM for your own files. Try one free plan for a month before paying.

Can I trust AI citations?⌄

Not blindly. Independent studies have found that a large share of AI citations do not fully support the sentence beside them, and some references are invented entirely. Treat every citation as a lead: open the page and read the sentence.

What is the difference between web search and deep research?⌄

Web search answers a question in seconds from a few pages. Deep research plans a search, runs several rounds of it, and produces a longer cited report, usually in five to thirty minutes. Use it for questions with several parts, not for a single fact.

How do I check whether a study an AI cited is real?⌄

Search for its title and authors, and check its DOI in a registry such as Crossref. The DOI must exist and belong to the study named. If you cannot find it, do not quote it.
