AI Transcription, Captioning and Content Repurposing as a Service

AI Tools & Online Business

By JoJo's Blocks · 2026-11-18 · 10 min read · transcription, repurposing

"We will not launch in June." That is what the speaker said. The AI transcript said, "We will launch in June." One missing word, and the whole sentence means the opposite. The client found it in the second paragraph, and that was the end of the job.

That is the service in one story. Every week, podcasters record hours of audio, businesses hold meetings nobody can search, and course creators film lessons that only exist as video — useful only if someone turns them into text people can read, search, and reuse. AI does the first pass in minutes. The part a client pays for is everything after that: checking every word, fixing the names and numbers AI gets wrong, and choosing what is worth keeping. Income is possible, but never guaranteed, and it starts small.

Three jobs under one name

Transcription turns audio or video into a written document. A podcast episode becomes a text page a reader can search. A meeting becomes a record with decisions and action items.

Captioning adds timed text to a video, so people can watch with the sound off or follow along when they cannot hear clearly. Captions are delivered as a file — usually SRT or VTT — that a video platform reads alongside the video.

Repurposing takes one long recording and turns it into several smaller pieces: three short clips for social media, five quotes for a newsletter, or a one-page summary. The recording is the raw material; the repurposed pieces are what the client actually publishes.

Start with one of the three. Transcription is the easiest entry point, because the workflow is the same every time.

Who buys this, and why

The buyers are people who record a lot and read little of what they record: podcasters who want episodes searchable, small businesses that record training calls, course creators who need captions, and coaches who want a summary of client calls. They buy for three reasons: time, search, and access. Reading takes a fraction of the time listening takes, search engines can find text but not audio, and captions make a video usable with the sound off — which, on social media, is most viewers.

The practice run: time yourself before you charge

Before you quote anyone a price, do this exercise three times. Take a recording you already have, run it through a transcription tool, and read the result while listening to the audio. Mark every error — a misheard name, a wrong number, a sentence that makes no sense — fix them all, and time yourself.

A clean 30-minute recording with two speakers takes a beginner 60 to 90 minutes to check properly, including the AI pass. That is the honest speed to plan around, and the number your prices have to be built on. Then repeat it with harder recordings: background noise, two people talking over each other. You will quickly learn which recordings to turn down — and turning one down beats delivering a bad transcript.

Otter.ai is built for meetings and live recordings. Descript lets you edit audio and video by editing its transcript, and exports captions. None of these tools is a substitute for listening. AI transcription is good on clear, slow speech in a quiet room, and still mishears names, numbers, and technical terms. It struggles with accents, overlapping speakers, and background noise. Treat every AI draft as a rough first version that needs a human to finish it.

The accuracy check is the real work

This is where a beginner earns the fee: a client does not pay for a button press, they pay for a transcript they can trust. Work through it while listening, and check five things.

1. Names. Every person, place, company, and product name, spelled exactly right — AI guesses at names, and a wrong one embarrasses the client.

2. Numbers. Prices, dates, and phone numbers — read them digit by digit. A $49 course written as $94 is a refund waiting to happen.

3. Speaker labels. Who said what, especially when two people sound alike — a decision given to the wrong person causes real confusion.

4. Meaning. A sentence the AI got grammatically right but logically wrong — "we will not launch in June" versus "we will launch in June" — is the most dangerous error of all.

5. Formatting. Clear paragraphs, timestamps where asked for, and "[inaudible]" where words cannot be heard, rather than a guess.

Do not clean up the speaker's words without being asked — a transcript is a record, not an essay. If the client wants filler words removed, agree that in advance.

For captions, the check is different. Read the caption file while watching the video: each caption should appear when the words are spoken, stay on screen long enough to read, and break at natural pauses. Keep lines short — about 42 characters at most — and no more than two lines at a time.

Repurposing: one recording, several useful pieces

Once the transcript is accurate, repurposing is mostly judgement: a 45-minute episode can become three clips, five quotes, and a one-page summary.

Start with the transcript, not the audio. Read it and mark the moments that stand alone: a clear answer to a common question, a short story, a surprising fact. Those are the clips. Then write the supporting text for each: a one-sentence caption in the client's own words, and a short post that says what the clip is about.

For short clips, Descript's editing-by-transcript approach works well: select the words in the transcript and the clip is cut for you. Use AI to suggest clips, but check each one yourself — listen to that part of the audio and make sure it does not cut a sentence in half. A clip that makes the speaker say something they did not mean is a trust problem, not a shortcut. If you want to turn clips into short-form video, see /blog/ai-short-form-video-side-hustle.html.

Agree the job in writing, and protect the recording

Before you start a paid job, agree four things in writing: what you will deliver, in what format (Word, PDF, or SRT file), by when, and for how much.

Confidentiality is part of the job. You will hear things that are not public: business plans, client names, personal stories, medical or legal details. Do not upload a client's recording to a tool that trains on uploaded content unless the client has agreed, do not share a recording or transcript with anyone else, and do not use a client's material in your own samples without written permission.

Also ask about consent: if people in a recording did not agree to a transcript being made, do not take the job until the client confirms they have the right to share it. One caution specific to Otter.ai: its privacy policy allows training on de-identified recordings and transcripts, and a privacy class action against the company was allowed to proceed in 2026 — read the tool's terms before you upload a client's meeting.

Pricing your first jobs, honestly

Charge by the audio minute, not by the hour, because the client can measure an audio minute and cannot measure your checking time. A common beginner structure is a per-minute rate for a clean transcript, with more for difficult audio, captions, or repurposing.

Work out the maths before you quote. A 30-minute recording at $1.00 an audio minute is $30 for the job; if it takes you 90 minutes to do it well, that is $20 an hour before any tool costs. A realistic beginner range for a clean AI-assisted transcript with a full human check is often around $1.00 to $1.50 an audio minute, with hard audio priced higher and captions adding roughly $0.50 to $1.00 on top. These are planning figures, not a rule: check what others charge in your market, and quote a fixed price for the whole job.

Compare that with the alternative the client already knows: Rev charges $1.99 a minute for a human transcript, so a 30-minute recording costs about $60 there. Your price needs to be lower than that to win the job, but high enough that the checking time is worth doing.

What it costs, and what you actually need to start

You can start for $0: the free plans above cover several sample jobs.

The real cost is time: a 30-minute recording takes roughly 60 to 90 minutes to check properly when you are starting out. One job, delivered well and on time, is worth more than five rushed ones.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Sending the AI draft without listening. The first draft always contains errors a client will find.
  • Guessing a name or a number. Mark it "[inaudible]" or ask the client. A wrong name is worse than a gap.
  • Promising a turnaround you cannot keep. A 60-minute recording is not a one-hour job. Quote the time it actually takes.
  • Uploading a client's recording without checking the terms. Some tools train on uploaded content. Check first, and ask the client if you are unsure.
  • Cutting a quote out of context. A clip that makes the speaker say something they did not mean is a trust problem, not a shortcut.

Conclusion

Transcription, captioning, and repurposing are honest beginner services because the work is visible: a client can read the transcript, watch the captioned video, and see whether the clips are accurate. AI makes the first pass fast, but it cannot hear what it misheard, check a number, or decide which moment is worth keeping. Start with transcription, learn the checking workflow on three sample recordings, and set a small, clearly priced first offer. Course creators are a natural first market — their lessons need transcripts and captions (see /blog/creating-small-online-course-ai-assistance.html).

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to be a fast typist to offer transcription?⌄

No. AI produces the first draft, and your job is to listen and correct it. Accuracy and patience matter more than typing speed.

How accurate is AI transcription?⌄

Good on clear, slow speech in a quiet room, and unreliable on names, numbers, accents, overlapping speakers, and background noise. That is why the human check is the service.

How much should I charge?⌄

There is no single right price. A common beginner range for a clean AI-assisted transcript with a full human check is around $1.00 to $1.50 per audio minute, with captions and repurposing on top.

What file format do clients need for captions?⌄

Usually SRT or VTT, which most video platforms accept. Ask the client which platform they publish on, and export in that format.
