Building a Simple Website with ChatGPT: What It Can Do, What It Can't, and a Beginner Workflow

AI Tools & Online Business

By JoJo's Blocks · 2026-11-18 · 10 min read · chatgpt, websites

You want a website: a page that says what you do, shows a few examples, and gives people one way to reach you. ChatGPT can help you build it, but only in a specific sense: it can plan the pages, write the words, draft the HTML and CSS, and help you fix an error. What it cannot do, in an ordinary chat, is put that website on the internet — there is no "publish" button in a normal ChatGPT conversation. This guide follows one fictional build from start to finish: Ana, a pottery teacher who runs weekend classes and needs one page with the class, the dates, the price, three photos, and an email link to book. Replace her details with yours, and the same workflow builds your site.

What ChatGPT can do — and what it cannot

Separate the work into two parts: making the site, and putting it online. ChatGPT helps with the first, not the second.

It can: plan a site's structure; write headlines, descriptions, and answers to common questions; draft HTML, CSS, and a little JavaScript; explain what each part does; and help you fix a problem when you paste in an error message.

It cannot: host or publish a site from an ordinary chat — the code lives in your conversation until you save it as files and upload them to a host. It cannot register a domain, log in to your accounts, or install WordPress. It cannot guarantee working or secure code: it can write code that looks complete and still has a mistake, a broken link, or a form that goes nowhere. And it cannot process payments, store orders, or keep a live site updated.

A note on ChatGPT Sites

ChatGPT Sites is a separate feature that can build, host, and share a simple website or small web app from a prompt. It has been in public beta since July 2026, and it is not the same as an ordinary chat. As confirmed in OpenAI's own Sites documentation, checked November 2026: Sites is not available on the Free or Go plans; it needs Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise, or Edu. Hosting is free through 31 December 2026, and from 1 January 2027 usage above each plan's monthly hosting allowance is billed — Plus includes 125 credits a month, Pro 375, Business 125 per seat. Building or editing a Site counts toward your plan's Codex usage, and a custom domain costs about 25 credits a month on top of what you pay the registrar. A new Site is private by default; where available, you can connect a domain you already own. Each Site's database is capped at 10 GB, and the documentation rules out payment-card data, protected health information, and financial transactions. The January 2027 rates are described by OpenAI as "proposed." Sites can suit a prototype or an event page. For a main business website, the ordinary route in this article is usually safer, because you keep your own copy of the files.

Step 1: Decide what the site is for

Do not open ChatGPT yet. Write one sentence: "This site helps [a specific person] to [do one thing] by [the main action]." Ana's: "This site helps parents nearby find a weekend pottery class and book a place." Then list the sections you need — usually Home, Services, Examples, Common questions, and Contact — and leave out any section that does not help a visitor decide or act.

Step 2: Gather your real content first

Before asking ChatGPT to write anything, collect your business name, one or two sentences about what you do, your services with real prices, two or three real photographs, and your contact details — only what you are happy to publish. This also protects you from ChatGPT's most common mistake: invented details. If you do not give it a price, it may invent one.

Step 3: Plan the site, then draft the words

Ask for a plan first, not a finished site. Give it your business type, your visitor, the one action you want, and your services with prices, then ask for the sections in order and three headline options — and tell it not to invent prices, places, or claims. Ana's prompt looks like this:

"I teach weekend pottery classes for beginners. Visitors are parents nearby deciding whether to book a Saturday class. The one action is to email me to book. Classes are Saturdays 10 am to 1 pm, $35 per person including materials. Please suggest the sections for a one-page website in order, and three headline options. Do not invent prices, dates, places, or claims I did not give you."

Read the plan as a stranger would, then ask it to draft one section at a time, using your facts. Edit by hand: read it aloud, cut slogans, and check every price, date, and name. If a stranger cannot say what you sell and what to do next in ten seconds, the words are not ready.

Step 4: Ask ChatGPT to write the code — and test it

Ask for a single HTML file with the styling included, using the text you approved, the sections in the order you agreed, and a contact section with your email address. Save the result as index.html. If ChatGPT gives you separate CSS or JavaScript files, save those too, in the same folder, with the exact filenames it uses — renaming a file can break the link to it.

Then open index.html in your browser. You should see your site, running locally on your own computer. Nobody else can see it yet — that is normal, and it is the right time to find problems. Check the text, click every link, and make the browser window narrow to see how the page reads on a phone. One warning: a form in a plain HTML file does nothing on its own. It needs a form service or a backend. Do not publish a form that silently goes nowhere — for a first site, a plain email link is more honest than a broken form. If something is wrong, describe it in plain words and ask for a corrected version. Test again until the page works on a computer and a phone.

Step 5: Choose how to publish it

This is the step ChatGPT cannot do for you. You need hosting (somewhere the files live) and, if you want your own address, a domain name.

GitHub Pages — free for public repositories, up to 1 GB per site with a soft bandwidth limit of 100 GB a month, custom domain and HTTPS included. One limit matters: GitHub says Pages is not intended or allowed as a free host for an online business, an e-commerce site, or a software service. Ana's class page fits; a shop would not.

Netlify Free — $0, with 300 credits a month as a hard limit for accounts created since September 2025. A deploy costs 15 credits and bandwidth costs 20 credits per GB, so 300 credits covers roughly 20 deploys, or about 15 GB of bandwidth, or a mix. When credits run out, sites pause until the next cycle, with no top-up on Free. Personal is $9 a month for 1,000 credits; Pro starts at $20 a month for 3,000. Netlify includes a custom domain with free SSL, allows commercial projects, and lets you drag a folder onto its site — the easiest start.

Carrd — a visual builder for one-page sites, with no code. Free is $0 for up to three sites on a carrd.co address, with "Made with Carrd" branding and no custom domain, forms, or analytics. Paid plans, billed yearly: Pro Lite $9, Pro Standard $19 (custom domain, forms, analytics), Pro Plus $49.

WordPress.com — the flexible option if you want a blog and several pages. Free is $0, with 1 GB of storage, a yoursite.wordpress.com address, and ads shown to visitors; no custom domain or plugins. Paid plans, billed yearly: Personal $4 a month ($48), Premium $8 ($96), Business $25 ($300), Commerce $45 ($540). Monthly billing costs more, and renewal prices are higher. Since April 2026, plugins are allowed on every paid plan.

The domain name is a separate purchase. For a standard .com, typical 2026 prices were roughly $10–$15 for the first year and $15–$22 a year to renew. A domain is rented by the year, not bought outright: if you do not renew it, you lose it.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Publishing a form that goes nowhere. Use an email link, or a tested form service.
  • Letting ChatGPT invent your details. Prices, dates, and qualifications must come from you.
  • Choosing the fanciest option first. A shop or booking system is a separate project. Start simple.
  • Forgetting the phone test. Most visitors will arrive on a phone.
  • Not keeping a copy. Save your files somewhere you control — a chat conversation is not a backup.

Conclusion

ChatGPT is a useful helper for building a simple website — as a planner, a writing assistant, and a coding assistant. It is not a website builder, a host, or a publisher. Decide what the site is for, gather your real content, plan the page with ChatGPT, draft and edit the words yourself, ask for the code, test it on a computer and a phone, then publish it through a hosting service you have chosen on purpose. A small site that is accurate, readable, and live is worth far more than a large site that exists only in a chat window. Frequently asked questions

Can ChatGPT build a website for free?⌄

Its free plan can draft the plan, the words, and the code. Going live is separate: you still need hosting, and a domain if you want your own address. A first site can be published for $0 on a free host.

Can ChatGPT publish my website?⌄

Not from an ordinary chat. ChatGPT Sites is a separate, paid-plan beta feature that can host a site, but it is not available on the Free or Go plans, and hosting above your plan's allowance is billed from 1 January 2027.

Which hosting option should a beginner choose?⌄

For a single HTML page, Netlify Free is usually the easiest start. For a one-page visual site with no code, Carrd is simpler. For a site that will grow into a blog, WordPress.com is more flexible.

What if ChatGPT gives me code that does not work?⌄

That is normal. Copy the error message, or describe what you see, and ask for a corrected version. If the same problem keeps coming back, simplify: publish the simple version first.
