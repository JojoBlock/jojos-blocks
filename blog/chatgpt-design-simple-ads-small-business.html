How to Use ChatGPT to Design Simple Ads for a Small Business

AI Tools & Online Business

By JoJo's Blocks · 2026-11-18 · 10 min read · chatgpt, ads

You have one afternoon, one offer, and no designer. That is enough to make a first ad — if you start with the offer instead of the tool. A small business does not need a design team: it needs one clear offer, one clear audience, and one image a person can understand in a few seconds. ChatGPT can help with the slow parts — a headline, a few lines of copy, a layout, a simple starting image — but it cannot replace your judgment, and it cannot guarantee clicks, visits, or sales.

This guide is a one-afternoon workshop: by the end you will have a brief, a headline tested on a real stranger, one finished ad file in Canva, and a plan to test it against a second version — all for $0 on free plans.

What ChatGPT can do — and what it cannot

ChatGPT is useful for drafting headlines and short descriptions, planning a layout, creating a simple starting image, and reading a draft as a first-time customer would.

It is not reliable for exact text inside an image — it often misspells words or changes a price, so add the final text in Canva instead. It does not know your colors, fonts, or logo, and it is a poor choice for real people and real products: an AI image of a cake is not your cake. For anything a buyer will judge by appearance, use a real photograph. ChatGPT can also invent a claim, so every fact must come from you. The rule: ChatGPT drafts, you decide.

The one-page brief: five answers before you open ChatGPT

Do not open ChatGPT and type "make me an ad." Start with five short answers: (1) the one offer — one product, service, or event; (2) who it is for — "parents nearby who need a birthday cake this weekend" is a brief, "everyone" is not; (3) what they should do next — visit, book, message, or click, pick one; (4) the one reason to care; (5) what must be true — the price, the dates, the location.

Here is a filled-in brief for a fictional plant shop, carried through this guide: a ready-to-gift herb set — basil, mint, and parsley — for $28, for people nearby buying a small gift for a friend who cooks. The action is to visit the shop on Saturday or message to reserve a set.

Step 1: Draft the copy in ChatGPT

Ask ChatGPT for copy — not a finished ad. Give it the brief and ask for options:

"I run a small plant shop. I am selling a ready-to-gift herb set — basil, mint, and parsley — for $28. It is for people nearby buying a small gift for a friend who cooks. The action is to visit the shop on Saturday or message us to reserve one. Please write five headlines of six words or fewer, three short descriptions of one or two sentences, and three calls to action. Use plain, friendly language. Do not add any claims I did not give you."

Ask for several options — the first headline is rarely the best — then edit the winner by hand and try the stranger test: show it to someone who does not know your business. If they cannot answer "what is it, and what do I do?" in ten seconds, the copy is not ready:

Before (ChatGPT's first draft)After (edited by hand)
"Elevate Your Culinary Journey with Our Premium Fresh Herb Collection Today!""A gift for someone who cooks. $28."
"Transform every meal with garden-fresh flavor.""Basil, mint, and parsley — ready to gift."
"Visit us today to discover the difference!""Visit Saturday, or message to reserve one."

The first version sounds like an ad. The second sounds like a shopkeeper talking to a neighbor — and it passes the stranger test.

Step 2: Make a simple image — or use a real photo

Use a real photograph when the product matters. If you sell food, plants, or handmade goods, a phone photo in good daylight, near a window on a plain surface, is usually better than an AI image.

Use a ChatGPT image when you need a simple scene or background, such as a calm illustration for a tutoring service. Describe the scene, not the ad: "Three small potted herbs — basil, mint, and parsley — in plain terracotta pots on a light wooden table, near a window, soft daylight, no text, no people, no logos." Check the result at full size for misspelled text; if it is wrong, ask for a version with no text, or use a real photo.

ChatGPT includes image generation on every plan, including Free, but the free allowance is limited and slower, and OpenAI does not publish a fixed number of images per day for any plan: Go gives you more than Free, Plus adds more complex and accurate generation, and Pro offers unlimited and faster generation, subject to abuse guardrails. The limit message inside your own ChatGPT account is the one that applies to you.

Step 3: Put the ad together in Canva

ChatGPT is a drafting tool. Canva is where the ad becomes a finished file, and its free plan is enough: start from a blank canvas in the right size, add your image, add your headline and description as real text, and add your logo. A reliable layout: the image at the top, about two-thirds of the space; the headline underneath; one or two sentences explaining the offer; and the price and the action at the bottom. Keep the text short, reuse the same colors and fonts from your Canva brand kit, and make the ad in the size for where it will appear — check the platform's own ad guide before you upload, because sizes change:

Where it will appearRecommended size

Step 4: Check the ad before anyone else sees it

Check the finished file, not your memory of it. Read every word, including the price — read the price backwards, digit by digit. Is the price right? Are the dates right? Does the image show what you actually sell? Is it clear what to do next, and is the link correct? Click it yourself, remove any word you cannot prove, and look at the ad on a phone before you pay.

One label to expect: Meta applies an "AI info" label to ads it detects were created or significantly edited with generative AI — including third-party tools, detected through industry signals such as C2PA metadata — shown in the ad's "About this ad" panel, more visibly for a photorealistic AI-generated person. Minor edits such as cropping do not trigger it. For ads about social issues, elections, or politics, the advertiser must disclose AI use themselves. This is a summary of Meta's published rules as of June 2026, not legal advice.

Step 5: Test two versions, then decide with evidence

Do not spend your whole budget on one ad. Make two versions that differ in one thing only — the same image with two different headlines, or the same headline with two different images. For our plant shop, version A could use "A gift for someone who cooks. $28." and version B "Basil, mint, and parsley — ready to gift." Run both briefly on a small budget and compare them on the same measure: clicks, or completions of the action you asked for. Change one thing at a time, give it enough time — a few hours is not a test — then keep the winner and test again. Keep expectations honest: a good ad usually brings a few inquiries or orders, not a transformation. If an ad does not work, the offer, audience, or message needs to change — not the tool.

Three examples to adapt

These are fictional. Replace every detail with your own. A local bakery: six seasonal muffins for $18, Saturday only. Headline: "Saturday muffins, ready by 9." Image: a real photo. Action: "Order by Friday evening." A tutoring service: a free 30-minute introductory session, online. Headline: "A calm first step in maths." Image: a simple illustration of a desk — no real child. Action: "Book a free first session." A handmade soap shop: a three-bar gift set for $24, posted within two days. Headline: "A small gift, ready to post." Image: a real photo of the bars, wrapped as they will arrive. Action: "Shop the gift set."

Mistakes to avoid

  • Letting ChatGPT write the price. It can invent a number. Type it yourself and check it twice.
  • Using an AI image of your product. A customer who receives something different will not come back.
  • Putting everything in the image. If the ad needs a paragraph to explain it, simplify the offer.
  • Testing everything at once. If two versions differ in more than one thing, you will not know what made the difference.

Conclusion

ChatGPT can make designing a simple ad faster, but it does not make the decisions for you: one offer, one audience, one action, and words and images that are true. Start with a short brief, draft the copy with ChatGPT, then edit it by hand — that edit is where the ad becomes yours. Use a real photograph when the product matters, assemble the ad in Canva in the right size, check the spelling, price, link, and platform rules, then test two versions on a small budget.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use ChatGPT to design ads for my business?⌄

Yes, for drafting copy, planning a layout, and creating simple starting images. Under OpenAI's Terms of Use (effective 1 January 2026), you own the output as between you and OpenAI — but it may not be unique, and you must not present it as human-made.

Do I need the paid version of ChatGPT to make ads?⌄

No. The free plan includes image generation, with a limited, slower allowance; OpenAI does not publish a fixed daily number. If you keep hitting the limit, Plus at $20 a month is the usual next step.

Can I use Canva for free to finish the ad?⌄

Yes. Canva Free is enough for a simple ad; Pro ($144 a year, or $18 a month) is worth it only if you make graphics often.

Will Meta label my ad as AI-generated?⌄

Meta may apply an "AI info" label to an ad it detects as AI-created or significantly edited. For social-issue, election, or political ads, the advertiser must disclose AI use.

Can ChatGPT make a logo for my business?⌄

It can generate an image that looks like a logo, but a logo needs to work at tiny sizes and in one color, and an AI-generated image may not be unique or protectable. Use a simple text-based logo in Canva, or work with a designer.
