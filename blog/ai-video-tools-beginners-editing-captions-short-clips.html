AI Video Tools for Beginners: Editing, Captions and Short-Form Clips

AI Tools & Online Business

By JoJo's Blocks · 2026-11-18 · 10 min read · ai-video, editing

Your first short video does not need a studio, a course, or a second mortgage in subscriptions. It needs a phone, one clear sentence, and about an afternoon. The sentence is the video. Everything else — the cuts, the captions, the clips — is just removing the parts that get in its way.

That is where AI video tools actually help. They are not directors. They are fast, tireless assistants for the boring jobs: cutting out the pauses, typing out the captions, and finding the two minutes inside a 45-minute recording that can stand on their own. This guide walks you through those three jobs in order, with the costs, the limits, and the one job — generating video from nothing — where beginners should slow down.

Your first video in an afternoon

Here is the whole job, before we get into tools. You are making a 40- to 60-second vertical video that answers one question or shows one thing. Write one sentence that states the promise, then write 100 to 130 words that deliver it. That is your script. Film it in one take, in daylight, with the light on your face or your subject, not behind it.

Then the edit takes roughly an hour the first time, and much less after that:

  1. Cut the pauses first. Remove silences, false starts, and filler words. This alone usually cuts a third of the length and makes the video feel twice as fast.
  2. Add captions, then read them. Auto-captions give you a draft. You give them a proofread.
  3. Add one quiet piece of music from the platform's own library, under your voice.
  4. Export vertical, 1080 × 1920 (9:16), at 1080p. Watch it on your phone with the sound off. If the point is not clear without sound, the captions or the visuals need work, not the music.

If that sounds doable, it is. The rest of this guide is choosing the tool that makes those four steps easiest for the way you record.

Step 1: Pick one editor and learn it properly

Do not sign up for four tools in your first month. Pick one, use it for ten videos, and only then judge it.

CapCut — the easiest start for short-form video. CapCut is a timeline editor built for vertical video, on phone, desktop, and web. You cut clips on a timeline, add captions, music, text, and effects, and export in the right size for each platform. It suits a beginner who records on a phone and posts Shorts, Reels, or TikTok. Its AI tools include auto-captions, background removal, and text-to-video, and the AI features run on credits on paid plans.

Descript — the best start if you talk to camera. Descript edits video by editing its transcript: delete a sentence and the video cuts itself. It suits tutorials and lessons, where the words matter most, and it has Studio Sound and filler-word removal. If you film your hands or your product rather than your face, CapCut is the better fit.

Step 2: Captions people can actually read

Captions are where most beginner videos fail, and they are the easiest thing to fix. Most viewers watch with the sound off, so captions are not optional — they are the video for a large part of your audience.

AI captions get the words roughly right and the details wrong, so the check matters more than the generation. Read every caption while watching the video. Each caption should appear when the words are spoken, stay on screen long enough to read, and break at a natural pause. Keep lines short — about 42 characters at most — and no more than two lines at a time.

Check the names, numbers, and prices first. AI captions mishear all three, and a caption with the wrong price is a wrong price. Use a plain, bold font in white with a dark outline or background, so the words stay readable over any part of the video. And keep captions in the middle of the frame: the top and bottom edges are covered by usernames, buttons, and platform captions, so text parked there disappears.

Step 3: Turn one long video into short clips

If you already record long videos — a lesson, a podcast, a talk — the cheapest way to make short-form content is to cut clips from them, not to record new ones.

Upload the long video to a clipping tool such as OpusClip. It finds the moments that stand alone — a clear answer to a common question, a short story, a surprising fact — reframes them vertically, adds captions, and gives each clip a score. A 45-minute recording can become three to five usable clips.

Then do the part the tool cannot do. Watch each clip yourself, from start to finish. Does it make sense without the rest of the video? Is the quote accurate, or does it cut a sentence in half? Is the speaker's face in frame, and are the captions readable? Pick the best two or three and throw the rest away. A clip that needs explaining is not a clip.

One warning about cost: OpusClip charges by the minute of source video you upload, not by the clip. A 60-minute recording costs 60 credits whether it produces five clips or twenty. Do not upload a two-hour recording to test the tool; upload a 15-minute section first.

Step 4: When to generate video with AI — and when not to

Tools such as Runway can turn a written description into a few seconds of video, or animate a still image. The results can be striking. They are also expensive per second, unreliable for anything that must be accurate, and easy to misuse.

Use generated video for a supporting shot: a background texture, a simple illustration in motion, or a scene that is hard to film. Do not use it for anything a viewer would take as real footage — a place, a person, an event, a product. A generated clip of a person doing something they never did is not a creative choice; it is a misleading video, and platforms treat it that way.

The cost is the other reason to be careful. Runway charges by the second: its main model, Gen-4.5, costs 12 credits a second, so a 5-second clip costs 60 credits. The free plan's 125 one-time credits buy about ten seconds of Gen-4.5 — enough to test the quality, not to make videos. The cheaper Gen-4 Turbo is listed at 5 credits a second in Runway's help center and API docs, but Runway's own plan cards imply about 6 credits a second, so do not budget on the Turbo rate until you see it in your own account.

The label rule, in plain English

All three platforms require a label when AI creates or meaningfully changes something realistic — a person, a place, an event, or a voice a viewer could mistake for real. On YouTube, you answer the "altered or synthetic content" question when you upload, and since May 2026 YouTube may also add the label itself. TikTok requires its label on realistic-looking AI scenes or people. Meta's rule covers photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio, including a Reel narrated with a realistic AI-generated voiceover.

What does not need a label: an AI-written script, captions, color grading, a cloned voice of your own, or clearly cartoon content. The test is realism, not whether you used AI at all. When in doubt, turn the label on.

There is a second rule worth knowing before you invest months in this. YouTube's monetization policies target "inauthentic" content — mass-produced, templated videos with no original insight, commentary, or value added. AI-assisted content is allowed; AI as the entire creative process, with nothing of yours in it, is not reliably monetizable. TikTok likewise keeps unoriginal or reused material without anything new added out of the For You feed.

What it costs, and what you actually need to start

You can start for $0. A phone camera, the free version of one editor, and the platforms' own music libraries are enough for your first month. US prices below were checked in November 2026; prices vary by country and device, so check the vendor's page before you pay.

Two details that catch people out. First, CapCut also sells a Standard plan at $9.99 a month in the US App Store — it is not listed on capcut.com's US pricing page, and its AI allowances are not published there. Second, credits are a real budget: CapCut Pro's 1,200 credits, Descript's media minutes, and OpusClip's source minutes all run out. OpusClip's paid credits roll over for up to two months, and its yearly Pro credits arrive as a lump sum. One editor is enough. If a tool costs $20 a month and you have not earned anything from the work it supports, pause it.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Editing before you have a script. A video with no clear point cannot be fixed in the edit. Write the one sentence the video promises, then record.
  • Trusting auto-captions without reading them. Names, numbers, and prices are where they fail first.
  • Generating video when recording is easier. A real 10-second clip of your product beats a generated one that took an hour and ten takes.
  • Uploading a two-hour video to a clipping tool on the free plan. You will spend the whole month's credits on one test.
  • Posting without the AI label when the video needs one. If a viewer could mistake AI video for real footage, turn the label on.

Conclusion

AI video tools are at their best on the slow, mechanical jobs: cutting pauses, adding captions, and finding the clips inside a long recording. You still choose the topic, write the script, record the video, check the captions, and decide whether a clip is honest enough to publish.

Start with one editor and one kind of video, post three useful clips a week for two months, and judge the work by whether people finish the video and come back.

Frequently asked questions

Which AI video tool should a beginner start with?⌄

The one that matches how you record. CapCut if you film on your phone and post short-form video; Descript if you talk to camera and the words matter most. Add a clipping tool only when you already have long videos to cut from.

Do I need the paid version of CapCut or Descript to start?⌄

No. Both have free plans that cover basic editing and captions. Only pay when you can name the limit you keep hitting — the number of media minutes, the export quality, or the AI credits.

Can AI captions be trusted?⌄

For a rough first draft, yes. For publishing, no — read every caption while watching the video, and check names, numbers, and prices first.

Do I need to label AI content in my videos?⌄

Yes, when it is realistic. If AI creates or changes a person, place, event, or realistic voice a viewer could mistake for real, turn on the platform's AI label. Scripts, captions, and cartoon-style content do not need one.
