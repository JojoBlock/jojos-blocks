Creating a Small Online Course with AI Assistance: Planning, Recording and Launching

AI Tools & Online Business

By JoJo's Blocks · 2026-11-18 · 10 min read · online-courses, ai-tools

Here is the arithmetic nobody shows you before you record a single lesson. Sell a $49 course on Teachable's Starter plan and you keep about $43.60 after the 7.5% platform fee and card processing — before the $29 monthly plan cost. Sell the same course on Udemy, where a buyer found it through search and paid a $14.99 flash-sale price, and you keep about $5.38. The course is identical. The platform and the price are what change.

That is why this guide starts with the money and ends with the recording. A small online course is one of the most honest digital products a beginner can make: you teach something you already know, in short lessons, and a student pays once to learn it at their own pace. It is also easy to get wrong: the work is front-loaded, and a long course nobody finishes is worse than a short, clear one.

AI can help with the slow parts: turning a messy outline into a clear lesson plan, drafting a script, or tidying up a recording. It cannot know your subject or decide what a beginner actually needs first. That part is yours. Income is possible, but never guaranteed, and most first courses sell slowly.

What counts as a small course

A small course teaches one skill, to one kind of beginner, in a few hours. "How to grow herbs on a balcony" is a course. "Gardening" is not. A good first course is usually 8 to 15 lessons, each 5 to 15 minutes long, adding up to about two to three hours in total, with something to do after most lessons: a worksheet or a short exercise.

Start with a subject you have done yourself, repeatedly — not something you read about last week.

Step 1: Pick one student and one result

Write down, in one sentence, who the course is for and what they will be able to do when they finish it. "Busy parents who pack school lunches will be able to plan five lunches in ten minutes on a Sunday evening." That sentence tells you what to include and what to leave out. Then test it: have you done this yourself, and can you name three mistakes beginners make? If not, narrow the topic.

Step 2: Check the idea before you build it

Spend an hour checking, not building. Search the platform where you plan to sell, using the words a buyer would type. Look at how many similar courses exist, what they cost, and what their reviews say — read the three- and four-star reviews first, because buyers often say what was missing.

Step 3: Outline first, then use AI to draft

Do the thinking before you ask AI for words. Write a one-page outline in your own words: the lessons, in the order a beginner would need them, with one sentence for what each lesson must teach and one thing the student will do.

Only then use AI as a drafting helper. Give it your outline and ask it to suggest a clearer order, spot a missing step, or draft a short script for one lesson you already understand. Check every suggestion: AI can invent a fact or suggest a step that does not work in practice.

A lesson plan that fits on one page is a good test. Take the lunch-planning course: Lesson 1, "The Sunday ten minutes" — teach the five-lunch grid, practice is filling in next week's grid. Lesson 2, "The five formulas" — teach one lunch formula per day of the week, practice is writing five lunches from the formulas. If a lesson cannot be described in two lines like that, it is two lessons hiding in one.

Step 4: Record simply, and check the sound first

You do not need a studio. A phone, good daylight, and a quiet room are enough for a first course. What matters most is sound: students forgive an ordinary picture, but they abandon a course they cannot hear clearly. Record a two-minute test first and listen to it on headphones and on a phone speaker.

Keep each lesson short and in one take where you can. If you stumble, pause, then repeat the sentence — you can cut the mistake out later. Film yourself, film your hands, or record your screen, whichever lets the student see what matters.

Use AI editing to save time, not to replace checking. Descript, for example, lets you edit a recording by editing its transcript. But AI captions still mishear names and numbers, so read the transcript of every lesson before you publish it. If you want to turn recordings into transcripts, captions and clips, see /blog/ai-transcription-captioning-repurposing-service.html.

Step 5: Build the lessons, with practice built in

A course is not a playlist of videos. Each lesson needs three parts: a short lesson, one thing the student does, and a way to know they did it right. The "doing" part is what makes a student finish — a worksheet, a photo of their work, or a three-question quiz is enough. Without it, most students watch two lessons and stop.

Keep the structure the same every lesson: what it teaches, the demonstration, the practice task, and a one-line summary. Add a short welcome lesson and a final lesson that tells them what to do next, and watch the whole course through as a student would.

Step 6: Choose one platform, and work out what you keep

Do not list the same first course on three platforms at once. Choose one. The four common routes are very different, and the cheapest depends on who finds your students. All figures below are US prices checked 18 November 2026; check the live pricing page before you buy.

Step 7: Price it, then launch small

Look at five similar courses and note their prices, then price by what your course does, not by what others charge. A short course of two to three hours sells for $29 to $79 on a hosted platform; on Udemy, the listed price matters less than the discounted price students actually pay. Pick a price you can defend in one sentence: "Two hours of lessons, six worksheets, and a skill you can use this week, for $49."

Publish one course, not three, and check it as a student would: enroll with a test account and try the practice task on your phone. Share it in one place where your students already are — a Pinterest board, a blog post, or a newsletter — with a short, honest description of who it is for.

Then answer your students — a course with an instructor who replies is worth more than a longer course with no one home. Keep a note of every question a student asks: those questions are the next lesson, or the next course.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Making the course too long. Ten lessons a student finishes beat thirty they abandon.
  • Recording before you outline. An outline takes an afternoon and saves days of re-recording.
  • Skipping the practice tasks. A course with nothing to do is a video playlist, and students can find those for free.
  • Choosing a platform for its headline price. A $0 monthly fee with a 10% cut, or a 37% share on discounted prices, can cost more than a $29 monthly plan once you sell regularly.
  • Treating AI output as finished. Your knowledge, your examples, and your checking are what make the course worth paying for.
  • Letting AI teach what you have not done. Do not teach a subject you have not done yourself, and do not copy another teacher's course — asking AI to rewrite it "in different words" is still copying.

Conclusion

A small online course with AI help is not a shortcut to income. It is a practical skill: choose one student and one result you can genuinely teach, check that someone wants it, outline the course yourself, use AI to draft and tidy, record simply with clear sound, build practice into every lesson, choose one platform after working out what you keep, and launch small. Frequently asked questions

Do I need to be an expert to make a course?⌄

You need real, repeated experience — enough to teach a beginner the steps, the mistakes, and how to fix them. AI cannot supply that experience.

Which platform is best for a first course — Teachable, Thinkific, Udemy, or Gumroad?⌄

It depends on who finds your students. Udemy brings buyers but keeps most of the money on organic sales. Teachable and Thinkific keep more per sale but need you to bring the students.

How long should my first course be?⌄

About two to three hours, in 8 to 15 lessons of 5 to 15 minutes each, with a practice task after most lessons. Shorter and finished beats longer and abandoned.

How much can I earn from my first course?⌄

There is no reliable figure. Most first courses earn nothing at first, then small amounts if the course is useful and people can find it.

Can AI write my whole course for me?⌄

No, not honestly. AI can draft an outline or tidy a script, but it cannot know your subject or teach from experience.
