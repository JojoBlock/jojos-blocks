AI Image Generation for Beginners: Choosing a Tool, Writing Prompts and Checking Licenses

AI Tools & Online Business

By JoJo's Blocks · 2026-11-18 · 10 min read · ai-images, licensing

A small business owner who needed an image used to have three choices: take a photo, buy a stock photo, or hire a designer. Now there is a fourth: describe the image in words and let an AI tool draw it in under a minute.

That is genuinely useful — and easy to get wrong. AI images can look convincing at a glance and fall apart on a closer look: extra fingers, misspelled words, a product that is not your product.

This guide answers three questions in order, because the order is the method: which tool fits the job, how do you write a prompt that gives you what you asked for, and what can you legally do with the result? Answer them backwards — generate first, ask later — and you will go wrong.

Question 1: Which tool fits the job?

No single tool is best at everything. Pick by what the image has to do, not by which tool is most talked about:

Do you need readable words inside the image? A poster, a sale banner, or a t-shirt draft with text on it? Start with Ideogram. Most image tools misspell words; Ideogram is the strongest common tool for readable text inside an image. Its free plan gives 30 credits a week to accounts signed in with Google, Apple, or Microsoft (email-and-password accounts get none), and everything made on it is public.

Does the image need to look like art? A book cover, a poster, or a product-page illustration where mood and color carry the message? Start with Midjourney, the tool most often praised for the look of its images: rich color, strong mood, images that feel designed. It has no free plan and no free trial.

Do you already use ChatGPT, or need to edit a photo you own? Start with ChatGPT Images, built into the same chat: describe the image, or upload a photo and ask for an edit. It is on every plan, including Free, but OpenAI describes the allowance in words, not numbers — "limited" on Free, "unlimited and faster" on Pro.

Do you need the image inside a design you are already building? Start with Canva Magic Media, which generates an image without leaving the Canva editor. For the full Canva workflow, see Canva AI for Social Media and Pinterest Graphics: A Beginner Tutorial.

What AI images are good for — and what they are not

AI image tools turn a written description into a picture. They are good at supporting images: a background texture, a simple illustration for a blog post, or a scene that is hard to photograph.

They are a poor choice for anything a buyer will judge by appearance. An AI image of a cake is not your cake, and an AI photo of a person wearing your shirt is not a real customer. For food, products, handmade goods, and real people, use a real photograph. They are also the wrong tool for exact facts: if the image must show a specific price, a real place, a logo, or a person's face, do not generate it.

Question 2: How do you write a prompt that works?

A good prompt is a brief, not a wish. Give the tool six things: subject, action, setting, style, light, and what to leave out. A plant shop wants an image for a post about caring for herbs:

"Three small potted herbs — basil, mint and parsley — in plain terracotta pots on a light wooden table, near a window, soft daylight, flat illustration style, warm colors, no text, no people, no logos."

Each part earns its place — and "no text, no people, no logos" rules out the three things AI images most often get wrong.

The prompt lab: three habits that fix most failures

Describe the scene, not the finished design. "A calm morning desk" gives the tool room to compose. "A Pinterest Pin with a headline in the top third" asks an image generator to do a designer's job, badly. Write the headline yourself, in real text, in Canva — never baked into the image.

Change one thing at a time. If the image is wrong, change one element — the style, or the light, or one object — and generate again.

Keep a note of prompts that worked. Save the prompt with the image file. Six months later, it is the only way to repeat the style.

Do not put a real person's name, a brand name, a trademarked character, or a famous logo in a prompt.

The five-point check: look at every image at full size

Check every image at 100% zoom before anyone else sees it. Five things to check:

Hands and faces. Extra fingers, a hand with the wrong number of joints, a face that looks almost right but not quite — the most common errors. If a hand looks wrong, crop it out or generate a new version.

Text. Read every word, letter by letter. AI tools still misspell words or invent words that look real at a glance. Never skip this check, even from Ideogram.

Numbers, shadows, and logic. Count the objects: three herbs, not four. Shadows pointing in two directions or a cup floating above a table are small impossibilities that make an image feel wrong.

Would a customer be misled? Would a reasonable person think it is a real product, place, or person? If yes, do not use it as if it were a photograph.

If an image fails, generate a new version, simplify the prompt, or use a real photo.

Question 3: What can you legally do with it? The license clinic

Three questions, in order. This is a summary of reported terms, not legal advice. For a specific product or dispute, ask a lawyer where you live.

1. Does the tool allow commercial use? Most paid plans do, but the details differ. OpenAI's terms say you own the output — but it may not be unique, and you must not present it as human-made. Midjourney says you own the assets you create, but companies earning over $1,000,000 a year must be on Pro or Mega to own them. Ideogram does not claim ownership of your outputs and does not restrict commercial use. Canva says you own the output and may use it commercially, but you cannot sell an AI image on its own. Read the terms of the tool you use before you sell anything made with it.

2. Do you own the copyright? Probably not — at least, not in the image alone. In the United States, copyright requires a human author. The US Copyright Office's January 2025 report concluded that prompts alone do not make the user the author, the D.C. Circuit agreed in Thaler v. Perlmutter (March 2025), and the Supreme Court declined to revisit it (2 March 2026). What you add can be protected: your own drawing, edits, or arrangement with your own text. A purely AI-generated image is unlikely to be protected. Do not build your brand's core asset — a logo, a book cover, a product's main image — from one unedited AI image.

3. Does the image copy someone else's work? An AI image can reproduce a recognizable style, a trademarked character, or a protected design. If the image looks like a famous character, a real person's face, or a well-known logo, do not use it. A free download is not a commercial license.

Putting the image to work

An image earns its keep when it does a job. Three common ones for a beginner.

A Pinterest Pin. Pinterest rewards tall images — the standard Pin is 1000 × 1500 pixels (2:3). Add your headline as real text in Canva.

A product mockup. An AI image can show a design on a mug or a t-shirt for a first listing, but replace it with a real photo of the printed product as soon as you have one.

What it costs, and when paying is worth it

You can start for $0: ChatGPT, Ideogram, and Canva all have free plans. US prices below were reported in October–November 2026, before tax. ChatGPT: Go $8, Plus $20, Pro from $100 a month. Midjourney: Basic $10, Standard $30, Pro $60, Mega $120 a month, about 20% less billed yearly. Ideogram: Plus $20 ($15 yearly), Pro $60 ($42 yearly) a month. Canva: Pro $144 a year or $18 a month.

Pay only when you can name the limit you keep hitting. Do not pay for three tools in your first month: one image tool and one design tool are enough.

Six mistakes to avoid

  • Using an AI image of your product. A customer who receives something different will not come back. Use a real photo.
  • Presenting an AI image as a real photo. An illustration is honest when it looks like one.
  • Skipping the full-size check. Hands, text, and shadows fail at full size, not as a thumbnail.
  • Assuming you own the copyright. A prompt alone does not make you the author under US law. Add your own work.
  • Copying someone else's work through a prompt. Asking for a famous character, logo, or brand "in different words" is still copying.
  • Paying for four tools at once. One tool, used well for a month, teaches you more.

Conclusion

AI image generation is at its best on small, specific jobs: a supporting illustration, a background texture, a first draft, or a Pinterest image in the right shape. It saves time by removing the slow work, not the thinking. You still choose the image, write the prompt, check the result at full size, and check the license before you publish or sell it.

Start with one tool you already have, one prompt formula, and one honest check: would a customer be misled by this image, and do I have the right to use it this way? If either answer is unsure, fix it first.

Frequently asked questions

Which AI image tool should a beginner start with?⌄

The one you already have — ChatGPT or Canva. Add Ideogram for readable text, and Midjourney when the artistic look is the point.

Can I use AI-generated images commercially?⌄

Usually yes, under the tool's terms — but the details differ by tool, and the terms change. Check them before you sell anything.

Do I own the copyright in an AI image I generated?⌄

Probably not in the image alone. In the US, copyright requires a human author, and prompts alone do not make you the author. What you add — your own artwork, edits, or arrangement with your own text — can be protected.

Why does my AI image have misspelled words or extra fingers?⌄

Because the tool predicts what a picture should look like; it does not understand words or count fingers. Check every image at full size.

Can I use an AI image as my logo?⌄

It is a poor choice. A logo needs to work at tiny sizes and in one color, an AI image may not be unique, and you are unlikely to own the copyright in it.
