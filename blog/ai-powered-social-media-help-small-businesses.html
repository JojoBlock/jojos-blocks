Offering AI-Powered Social Media Help to Small Businesses

AI Tools & Online Business

By JoJo's Blocks · 2026-11-18 · 10 min read · social-media, small-business

It is Tuesday morning, and Rosa has been up since 5 a.m. Her bakery's Instagram account has not been touched in three weeks. Her phone holds a photo of Friday's sourdough, a customer asking whether she delivers, and a holiday-hours notice she meant to post last month.

That gap is the job: showing up every week for one or two small businesses, turning what the owner already knows into posts that are true, and answering the customers who write in. AI does the slow drafting. You do the learning, the checking, and the showing up.

The job, in one sentence

You help a small business post regularly, accurately, and in its own voice — three posts a week, approved in advance, scheduled on time.

The businesses that need this most have one to ten staff and no marketing person: cafes, salons, tradespeople, independent shops, tutors, local services. They have photos on their phones and knowledge in their heads, and no system for turning either into posts. A small business does not need a viral video. It needs a post that says it is open and what it sells, a post that answers a question customers keep asking, and someone who replies when a customer writes in.

Your first client: a week with Rosa's bakery

Imagine Rosa says yes to a one-month trial: three posts a week on Facebook and Instagram.

On Monday she sends three photos and three facts: Friday sourdough is back, $6 a loaf; the shop closes early on Saturday; a customer asked whether she makes gluten-free bread (she does not). On Tuesday you draft the three captions with AI help, rewrite each until it sounds like Rosa, and check every price and day. On Wednesday she approves two and corrects the third — the wedding is Saturday, not Friday. On Thursday the posts go out. On Friday you check the accounts once, answer the delivery question from her approved answers, and pass the catering question to her the same day.

Five services you can genuinely offer

Offer one or two to start.

1. A weekly content calendar. Three posts a week, planned a month ahead around what the business actually does. You draft; the business approves.

2. Writing captions. The owner sends a photo and a sentence — "new sourdough on Fridays, $6 a loaf" — and you turn it into a clear caption with the price, the day, and what to do next.

3. Simple graphics in Canva. A price list, a holiday-hours notice, a Pinterest pin. Use the business's real photos, and put real text in Canva rather than text baked into an AI image, so nothing is misspelled. For a fuller guide to Canva graphics, see /blog/canva-ai-social-media-pinterest-graphics.html.

4. Scheduling and publishing. You load approved posts into a scheduler so they go out on time. Meta Business Suite schedules Facebook and Instagram for free — Page posts can be scheduled from 20 minutes to 29 days ahead — and Buffer covers more platforms on its free plan. Someone still has to watch for comments.

5. Replying to comments and messages. Many small businesses lose customers by leaving a question unanswered for four days. Offer to check once a day and reply to the routine ones — opening hours, directions, "do you have this in stock?" — from answers the owner approved in advance. Anything you cannot answer, pass to the owner the same day.

Learn the business before you write a word

AI can draft a caption in seconds. It cannot know that the bakery closes on Mondays or that the plumber does not cover the next town. Every mistake like that costs the business a customer.

Before you write anything, ask the owner for the facts and write them down on one page: opening hours, what they sell and what they do not sell, prices for the five most popular items, and how to book or order. Ask for five real photos you may use, and which past posts they liked.

Then write three sample posts using only the facts on your page, and show them to the owner. If they say "that sounds like us," you have the voice right.

The weekly workflow

Use the same steps for every client, every month.

1. Agree the scope in writing. How many posts a week, on which platforms, who approves, and what the monthly price is. Put it in a short email, not a handshake. Nothing is published without the owner's approval until you have worked together for at least a month.

2. Gather material once a week. Ask the owner for three photos and three facts from the week.

3. Draft with AI, then rewrite by hand. Give the AI the facts, ask for two or three caption options, then rewrite the best one until it sounds like the business. Check the price, the day, and the opening hours. If the AI added a claim the owner did not give you — "the best coffee in town," "sale ends Sunday" — cut it.

4. Send the week for approval. One message, all the posts, photo and caption together: "Here are this week's three posts. Reply 'yes' and I will schedule them, or tell me what to change."

5. Schedule, then watch. Load the approved posts into the scheduler, then check the accounts once a day. Reply to routine comments from the approved answers, and tell the owner about a genuine complaint instead of deleting it.

6. Report once a month, honestly. One short message: how many posts went out, which ones got the most saves, comments, or messages, and what you suggest next month.

The rules that catch beginners out

Label realistic AI content. If a post uses a photorealistic AI video or a realistic-sounding AI voiceover, the platform may require its AI label. Meta's help pages say so for Facebook and Instagram, for video or audio that was digitally created or altered, including with AI. That requirement does not apply to images, though Meta may still label them itself. Scripts, captions, color grading, and cartoon-style content do not need a label. The test is realism, not whether you used AI at all.

Paid posts are labeled too. Meta automatically applies an "AI info" label to ads it detects as AI-created or edited, and advertisers must self-disclose AI use only for ads about social issues, elections, or politics. If a post is a paid partnership or an ad, say so clearly — use the platform's paid partnership label and plain words such as "#ad".

Never fake engagement. Do not buy followers, use bots to like or comment, or post fake reviews or testimonials. A client who discovers you inflated their numbers will not keep you, and a platform that detects it can restrict the account.

Never post a claim you cannot verify. If the owner did not tell you the price, the date, or the offer, do not write it. If a photo is AI-generated, do not present it as a real photo of the product, the shop, or the owner.

You do not own the accounts. The client owns their Facebook Page, their Instagram account, and their photos. Never change a password without being asked, and never lock the owner out of their own account.

Pricing your first clients, honestly

There is no standard price for this work, and anyone who gives one number for every beginner is guessing. Price by what you will actually do each week, not by what the tools cost. A fair first offer is a small monthly fee for a clearly defined job: three posts a week on two platforms, written from the owner's photos and facts, scheduled and approved in advance, with comments checked once a day. Ad spend is paid by the client, never by you.

Work out what the month is worth to you in hours. If three posts a week plus daily checking takes four hours a week, that is about sixteen hours a month. If the fee works out to less than you would accept for sixteen hours of careful work, the price is too low or the job is too large — make the job smaller before you make the price smaller.

What it costs, and what you actually need to start

You can start for $0. Meta Business Suite schedules Facebook and Instagram posts for free. Buffer's free plan covers up to three channels with ten scheduled posts per channel at a time — a queue limit that refills as posts go out. Its Essentials plan costs $6 per channel per month billed monthly, or $5 billed yearly, so two channels cost $12 a month. ChatGPT Plus costs $20 a month and Canva Pro $144 a year or $18 a month, in the US — but both have free plans, and you should start free.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Posting without approval. One wrong price or one unwanted photo can end the relationship in a day.
  • Writing in your voice, not the business's. A bakery does not sound like a tech startup.
  • Promising reach or sales. You cannot promise views, clicks, or customers. Promise regular posts, checked facts, and honest reporting.
  • Taking on five clients at once. One client, done well for two months, teaches you the work and gives you a reference.
  • Treating AI output as finished. The AI drafts; you decide. If you would not sign your name to a post, do not publish it under the client's name.

Conclusion

Offering social media help to small businesses is not about the tools. It is about being the person who shows up every week: learning the business, writing posts that are true, getting them approved, scheduling them on time, and answering customers politely. AI makes the drafting faster, but it cannot learn the business, check the price, or earn the owner's trust. Start with one local business, one small monthly offer, and three posts a week done properly. Frequently asked questions

Do I need experience to offer social media help to small businesses?⌄

No, but you need to be organized, careful with facts, and willing to learn one business properly.

Should I tell the client I use AI?⌄

Yes, if they ask, and ideally before you start.

Do I need to label AI content in a client's posts?⌄

If a post uses photorealistic AI video or a realistic AI voiceover, the platform may require its AI label. Scripts, captions, and cartoon-style content do not need one.

How much should I charge?⌄

There is no single right price. Work out the hours the job takes each month and quote a fixed monthly fee for a clearly defined job.

Can I manage a client's accounts from my own phone?⌄

Only with their permission, and only through the proper access — for example, being added to their Facebook Page in Meta Business Suite. Never ask for, or keep, a client's password.
